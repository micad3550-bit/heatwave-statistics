How much did climate change matter to this heat wave?

A record heat wave is on the wire and your editor wants the climate angle. You can’t write that climate change “caused” it. You can often write how much it changed the odds. This page lets you test that sentence before you file it.

The decision-maker: a reporter covering a record heat wave, on deadline.

The decision: what, if anything, can I say about climate change’s role, and how strongly can I say it, without overclaiming?

Course concept: extreme event attribution, measured as relative risk: how many times more likely an event became.

Four ideas behind the sentence

Attribution scientists ask a narrow question: how often would a heat wave at least this hot have happened in a world without human-caused warming, compared with now? Four measures come out of that comparison, and each one belongs in a different kind of sentence.

Chance in any year, then and now
The odds that one year’s hottest day reaches this level or higher, in the 1850–1900 climate (then) and in today’s (now). Often quoted as “1 in 100 years.”pthen and pnow
Relative risk
How many times more likely the event is now. “10 times more likely” means a relative risk of 10. Attribution studies often call it the probability ratio.RR = pnow ÷ pthen
Share of the risk due to climate change
Of every 100 heat waves this extreme in today’s climate, how many would not have happened in the old climate. Also called the fraction of attributable risk.1 − 1 ÷ RR
Intensity change
How much cooler a heat wave with the same odds would have been in the old climate. It answers “how much hotter?” where relative risk answers “how much more likely?”change in °C at equal odds

Try the arithmetic

Each year’s hottest day is a draw from a bell curve. Warming slides the curve toward higher temperatures. The record is a vertical line, and the shaded area to its right is the chance of a hottest day that hot or hotter. Move the sliders and watch what happens to the odds.

Sliders

Defaults describe a typical land location today. Every range and its source are listed under “Where the numbers come from.”

The event

Degrees above the typical hottest day of the year in 1850–1900. 0 is an ordinary hottest day; 5 or more smashes old records.

The place

The standard deviation of the yearly hottest day. Steady tropical coasts sit low; continental interiors sit high. 1.8 °C reproduces the IPCC’s land-wide numbers.

The climate

How far warming has slid the whole curve. 1.8 °C matches today’s global warming (about 1.4 °C) as it plays out over land. About 2.6 °C matches a 2 °C world.

0% means the curve only slides. A positive value means it also widens, which fattens the hot tail. Some regions show this; many analyses assume none.

        What to notice as you move the sliders

        • Rarer records gain the most. Hold warming fixed and raise the record. Relative risk climbs steeply because the shift pushes a bigger share of years over a high bar. The curve in the second chart bends upward for this reason.
        • The same warming means more in a steady climate. Pull the variability slider down. A 1.8 °C shift is a modest nudge where hot days swing by 3 °C and a huge one where they swing by 1 °C. Try the tropical case.
        • The far tail is fuzzy. Push the record up until the odds then fall below 1 in 1,000. The band in the second chart fans out and a warning appears. This is why published studies say “at least.”
        • The spread matters on its own. Set warming to 0 and widen the spread. Relative risk still rises. Attribution studies test this because it can change the answer.

        What you can defensibly write

        The box above the charts rewrites itself as you move the sliders. These habits hold at any setting.

        Safe

        • Human-caused climate change made a heat wave like this about X times more likely.
        • …and about Y °C hotter than it would have been in the 1850–1900 climate.
        • “At least” wording when the event is far out in the tail.
        • Name the study, the region and the exact event definition (hottest day or multi-day average).

        Avoid

        • “Climate change caused this heat wave.” Weather has many causes; attribution measures a change in odds.
        • A single exact number with no range or “at least.”
        • Treating “91% of the risk” as “91% of the heat.” It describes odds, not temperature.
        • Quoting this page. It is a classroom model. Quote a peer-reviewed or rapid-attribution study of your event.

        Where the numbers come from

        The model treats each year’s hottest day as a normal (bell-curve) draw. Climate change shifts the average by the warming slider and can change the spread. The defaults are not guesses: we fitted this model to the IPCC’s published hot-extreme numbers.

        Check 1: does the model reproduce the IPCC?

        The IPCC (AR6, Summary for Policymakers, Figure SPM.6) gives, for a hot extreme over land that happened once in 10 years in 1850–1900, how much more often and how much hotter it gets at each global warming level. Fitting a bell curve to those four rows gives a spread of about 1.8 °C and a local shift of about 0.71 spreads per °C of global warming (1.28 °C of local shift per °C of global warming). Those fitted values are the slider defaults.

        The math and the model’s limits

        Let E be the record’s height above the 1850–1900 mean, σ the spread then, ΔT the warming shift and c the change in spread. With Q the upper tail of the standard normal:

        p_then = Q( E / σ )
        p_now = Q( (E − ΔT) / (σ(1+c)) )
        RR = p_now / p_then
        share of risk = 1 − 1 / RR
        intensity change = E − σ · (E − ΔT) / (σ(1+c))

        Limits to say out loud: real hottest-day data are not perfectly bell-shaped, and attribution studies use extreme-value statistics and climate models. Far-tail results swing a lot with the assumed shape, which is why we cap the language and show a sensitivity band. The model also says nothing about why a particular heat dome formed. And a station or region needs its own event definition (hottest day, three-day mean, area average) because the answer changes with it.
